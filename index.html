A CLEAR PLACE TO START

Before you respond
to that letter.

A motoring notice can have consequences beyond the original allegation.

What begins as a speeding allegation can develop into a fixed penalty, a request to identify a driver, a court prosecution or a potential driving disqualification. Each stage involves different documents, deadlines and procedural requirements.

You may also have received a letter from the magistrates' court, a Single Justice Procedure Notice, a financial penalty notice or correspondence concerning an existing driving ban.

I examine the paperwork, the relevant dates, the circumstances and the information available. The purpose is to establish what has happened, what the documents actually require and whether any genuine issue warrants further investigation.

Not every notice is defective. Not every allegation has a viable defence. If the documents appear correct, I will explain that rather than suggest a challenge without proper grounds.

FROM THE FIRST NOTICE TO THE NEXT STAGE

One allegation can produce several documents.

Understanding which document you have received is important. A request for driver details is not the same thing as a fixed penalty or a court charge.

Ask about your paperwork →

Notice of Intended Prosecution (NIP)

A notice informing a person that prosecution for a specified road traffic offence is being considered. The date of the alleged offence, service details and statutory requirements may be relevant to the assessment.

Section 172 notice

A request under section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988 requiring information about the identity of the driver. The recipient, vehicle details, response instructions and deadline should be checked carefully.

Fixed Penalty Notice (FPN)

A fixed penalty document setting out the alleged offence and the available response. The amount, payment instructions, penalty points and consequences of accepting the offer should be understood before responding.

Single Justice Procedure Notice (SJPN)

A notice used for certain criminal proceedings that may be decided by a magistrate without a conventional courtroom hearing. It contains a charge and response instructions. The response period is particularly important.

Written charge and requisition

Documents identifying a prosecution and requiring the recipient to respond or attend court. The charge, hearing arrangements and accompanying information need to be read together.

Statement of facts and prosecution evidence

Material setting out the prosecution's account of the allegation. Depending on the case, relevant material may include officer statements, photographs, technical records and other evidence.

Sentencing notice and court order

Documents recording a court's decision, financial penalties, penalty points, disqualification and any other requirements imposed by the court.

Enforcement correspondence

Letters relating to unpaid financial penalties, collection activity, enforcement agents, payment arrangements or other action following a court order.

COURT AND DISQUALIFICATION

Already facing court? The details matter.

A court summons, conviction or driving disqualification requires more than looking at the original speeding notice.

I can examine the court paperwork, explain what the documents mean and identify questions that may require further investigation or specialist legal representation.

Explore court and ban matters →

Totting-up disqualification

A driver who reaches 12 or more penalty points within three years will generally face disqualification under the totting-up provisions, subject to the applicable law and circumstances. The existing record, relevant dates and court documents matter.

Exceptional hardship

A driver facing a totting-up ban may ask the court to consider exceptional hardship. The court assesses the evidence and circumstances. Ordinary inconvenience is not automatically exceptional hardship.

Magistrates' Court proceedings

Review of written charges, requisitions, hearing dates, prosecution documents, plea instructions and other court correspondence.

Single Justice Procedure

Assessment of Single Justice Procedure Notices, the charge, statement of facts, plea options and response requirements. The notice normally gives 21 days to respond.

Disqualification orders

Examination of the wording of a driving ban, the effective date, duration, licence requirements and any instructions imposed by the court.

Appeals and post-conviction matters

Court decisions, sentencing documents, appeal information and questions concerning the next procedural steps. Applicable appeal deadlines can be short.

Unpaid fines and enforcement

Financial penalty notices, payment records, enforcement letters, outstanding balances and correspondence following a court decision.

FINES, PENALTY POINTS AND LICENCE RECORDS

The financial penalty is not the only consequence.

A motoring conviction can affect a person's driving record, insurance position and ability to continue driving.

Fixed penalties

A fixed penalty is an alternative to prosecution in circumstances where it is offered. The document should be examined for the alleged offence, amount, response instructions and any associated endorsement.

Court-imposed fines

A court fine is different from a fixed penalty. The court determines the financial penalty in accordance with the offence, applicable sentencing rules and relevant circumstances.

Penalty points and endorsements

Endorsement codes identify the offence recorded on the driving record. Different offences carry different point ranges and endorsement periods.

Totting-up consequences

Accumulating 12 or more points within three years can lead to a driving disqualification. New drivers may also be affected by separate licence-revocation rules.

Outstanding court balances

A fine balance may be accompanied by enforcement fees, collection correspondence or other action. The original order and subsequent records should be considered together.

Driving entitlement after a ban

Whether a person needs to apply for a new licence, take a test or satisfy another requirement depends on the disqualification and the instructions from the court and DVLA.

IMPORTANT INFORMATION BEFORE YOU ACT

A few things worth checking immediately.

If you have received a motoring notice, there are some basic steps that can help preserve the relevant information.

What to keep

  • The original notice and every page supplied with it.
  • The envelope, postal markings and delivery information.
  • Any photographs, camera images or evidence supplied.
  • Copies of letters, emails and replies already sent.
  • Proof of postage, online submissions or payments.
  • Any court order, hearing notice or enforcement letter.
  • A written record of relevant dates and conversations.

What not to overlook

  • Do not ignore a Section 172 driver identification request.
  • Do not assume an enquiry pauses a statutory deadline.
  • Do not ignore a court hearing or Single Justice notice.
  • Do not assume a late-arriving letter is automatically invalid.
  • Do not drive while subject to an active disqualification.
  • Do not assume an unpaid fine will simply disappear.
  • Do not submit inaccurate information or alter evidence.

An important note about deadlines

Different notices have different response periods. For example, a Section 172 request generally requires a response within 28 days, while a Single Justice Procedure Notice normally allows 21 days. These are not interchangeable deadlines. Read the actual document and obtain appropriate advice promptly.
