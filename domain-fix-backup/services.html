UNDERSTANDING YOUR DOCUMENTS

One letter can lead to a much bigger problem.

The first document is not always the last.

A camera-detected speeding allegation may begin with a Notice of Intended Prosecution and a request for driver information. It may then lead to a fixed penalty or prosecution.

Other matters begin with a roadside stop, a request for documents, an allegation involving a mobile phone or a notice concerning an existing driving record.

A court case can introduce further paperwork: a written charge, requisition, Single Justice Procedure Notice, prosecution evidence, sentencing notice or financial penalty order.

My work starts with identifying the actual document, understanding its purpose and examining the information available.

01

SPEED LIMIT ALLEGATIONS

Speeding notices and allegations

A speeding allegation can arise from a fixed camera, mobile camera, average-speed system or an officer's observation. The paperwork that follows depends on how the alleged offence was detected.

Where a camera detects an alleged offence, the registered keeper may receive a Notice of Intended Prosecution and a Section 172 request. The driver identification process and the subsequent response are separate parts of the procedure.

I examine the notice, the alleged speed, the location, the relevant dates and the documents supplied. Where available and relevant, camera photographs and technical records may also need to be considered.

What can be examined?

  • The alleged speed and applicable speed limit.
  • The road, location and direction of travel.
  • The date and time recorded on the notice.
  • Service and correspondence records.
  • Available camera photographs and supporting material.
  • Relevant technical and evidential documentation.
  • Any previous response or representation.

Possible consequences

GOV.UK states that the minimum speeding penalty is generally Â£100 and three penalty points. More serious allegations can lead to higher penalties, court proceedings or disqualification.

The actual outcome depends on the speed, applicable limit, circumstances and sentencing rules.

02

NOTICE OF INTENDED PROSECUTION

NIPs and the fourteen-day requirement

A Notice of Intended Prosecution informs a person that prosecution for a specified road traffic offence is being considered. It is commonly referred to as a NIP.

Section 1 of the Road Traffic Offenders Act 1988 contains requirements concerning warning or service for certain offences. In many camera-detected cases, the first notice must be served within fourteen days on the registered keeper at the time.

The fourteen-day rule is not a universal cancellation rule. Statutory exceptions exist. The registered keeper may also have changed, or the vehicle registration information may have been inaccurate.

The date printed on a letter is not always the same as the date it was received. The method and circumstances of service can matter.

Matters examined

  • Date of the alleged offence.
  • Date of issue and available service records.
  • Identity of the registered keeper at the relevant time.
  • Address and vehicle registration information.
  • Previous notices and any changes of keeper.
  • Whether an applicable statutory exception may arise.

Relevant legislation

Section 1 and section 2 of the Road Traffic Offenders Act 1988 should be considered where the statutory warning requirements are in issue.

Questions worth establishing

Who was the registered keeper? The position at the time of the alleged offence.
When was the notice served? The relevant service date and available evidence.
Was there an earlier warning? A roadside warning may be relevant.
Was an exception applicable? The statutory exceptions must be considered.

A notice received after fourteen days is not automatically invalid. The complete circumstances must be examined.

03

DRIVER IDENTIFICATION

Section 172 requests

A Section 172 notice is a formal request for information about the identity of a driver. It is commonly sent alongside a camera-detected Notice of Intended Prosecution.

Section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988 places duties on vehicle keepers and certain other people to provide information that may identify the driver.

A written request generally carries a twenty-eight-day response period, calculated from service of the notice. The precise requirements and circumstances should be checked.

Failing to respond can create a separate offence, even where the original allegation was speeding. This is why the identification request should not be confused with the underlying speeding allegation.

What we examine

  • Who received the request.
  • The vehicle and alleged offence details.
  • The response instructions and service date.
  • Whether a response was submitted.
  • Available proof of posting or online submission.
  • Any genuine difficulty identifying the driver.
  • Subsequent prosecution correspondence.

Relevant provision

Section 172 of the Road Traffic Act 1988. The statutory duty and available exceptions depend on the recipient and circumstances.

04

FIXED PENALTIES AND ENDORSEMENTS

Fines, penalty points and licence records

A Fixed Penalty Notice offers a prescribed penalty for certain road traffic offences. It may involve a financial penalty, penalty points or other consequences.

Some fixed penalties are issued following a roadside stop. Others follow a camera investigation and the identification of the driver.

The document should be read carefully. The alleged offence, penalty amount, endorsement and response instructions all matter.

What can be examined?

  • The offence recorded on the notice.
  • The penalty amount and payment instructions.
  • The points or endorsement associated with the offence.
  • Eligibility for a fixed penalty offer.
  • Any earlier driving record information.
  • Whether court proceedings have subsequently begun.

Understanding penalty points

Different motoring offences carry different endorsement codes and penalty point ranges. The actual number of points depends on the offence and the applicable rules.

Accumulating twelve or more points within three years can lead to totting-up disqualification. New drivers are subject to a separate licence-revocation scheme.

HANDHELD DEVICE ALLEGATIONS

Mobile phone offences

Using a handheld mobile phone or another relevant device while driving can result in prosecution. The rules also cover certain devices capable of sending and receiving data.

The law contains specific provisions concerning holding and using devices, and separate requirements concerning control of a vehicle.

The circumstances of an allegation matter. A person may be stopped by an officer, or an allegation may be supported by other evidence.

What can be examined?

  • The precise offence alleged.
  • Officer statements and accounts.
  • Available photographs or video.
  • The description of the device and alleged use.
  • Relevant witness evidence.
  • The notice, charge and prosecution documents.

Potential penalties

GOV.UK states that holding and using a relevant device while driving can result in six penalty points and a Â£200 fine. Court proceedings can carry further consequences.

New drivers who passed their test within the previous two years can face licence revocation under the applicable rules.

06

DOCUMENTARY AND TECHNICAL MATERIAL

Evidence and procedural review

A prosecution document describes an allegation. Supporting evidence is what the prosecution relies upon to establish it.

Depending on the case, that evidence may include photographs, video recordings, officer statements, technical records, certificates and other material.

I can examine supplied documents, identify inconsistencies or unanswered questions and explain where further material may be required.

Material that may be relevant

  • Speed camera photographs.
  • Officer witness statements.
  • Device and equipment records.
  • Calibration or testing documentation, where relevant.
  • Vehicle identification information.
  • Location and timing records.
  • Postal and service evidence.
  • Previous correspondence and representations.

What the review can establish

The review can identify what material has been supplied, whether it addresses the allegation and which questions remain unanswered.

A document review is not a substitute for forensic testing, independent expert evidence or formal disclosure procedures. Those may require specialist professionals.

ALCOHOL AND DRUG-RELATED ALLEGATIONS

Drink-driving and drug-driving matters

Drink-driving and drug-driving allegations can involve serious criminal proceedings, substantial financial penalties and driving disqualification.

The documents may include roadside procedure records, breath test information, evidential breath specimens, blood or urine results, laboratory material and officer statements.

The particular allegation matters. Driving with excess alcohol, driving while unfit through drink or drugs, and failing to provide a specimen are distinct offences.

Documents that may be examined

  • Charge sheets and prosecution notices.
  • Roadside testing records.
  • Evidential breath records.
  • Blood or urine specimen documentation.
  • Laboratory certificates and reports.
  • Officer statements and interview records.
  • Court orders and disqualification documents.

Important limitation

These matters require careful legal attention. A document review does not determine intoxication, provide medical evidence or replace specialist criminal defence representation.

AN IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

A review is not a guarantee of a defence.

A responsible assessment must recognise the limits of the available information. A notice can be correctly issued and a prosecution can have sufficient evidence.

Where there is no viable issue to raise, that should be explained. Where a matter requires specialist legal representation, it should be referred appropriately.

No guaranteed cancellations No notice is promised to be withdrawn or overturned.
No invented procedural defects A genuine legal or evidential basis is required.
No replacement for solicitors Reserved legal work must be undertaken by authorised professionals.
No automatic deadline extensions An assessment does not suspend statutory or court deadlines.
No unsupported evidence claims Missing or unavailable material cannot be treated as proof of a defect.
