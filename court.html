A serious stage

Know what the court is asking you to do.

A court notice can arrive after a speeding allegation, an alleged failure to identify a driver, a mobile phone offence or another motoring matter. It may ask you to enter a plea, attend a hearing or provide information.

The first task is to identify exactly what document you have received. A Single Justice Procedure Notice is not the same as a hearing notice, and a request for information is not the same as a conviction or sentence.

Do not assume that a letter is routine simply because the allegation concerns a traffic offence. A missed response date can have serious consequences.

This page provides general information for England and Wales. It is not a substitute for advice from a qualified legal professional about your individual case.
Start with the paperwork

Which document has arrived?

The heading, issuing authority, allegation and response date help establish where the case is in the process. Keep every page and envelope.

01

Single Justice Procedure Notice

Used for certain minor criminal offences that may be decided by a magistrate without a traditional court hearing. The notice explains how to respond and enter a plea. The usual response period is 21 days from the date shown on the notice.

02

Summons or requisition

A document requiring you to attend court or otherwise respond to a criminal allegation. Read the hearing details carefully and check whether attendance is required.

03

Written charge and prosecution papers

These may set out the offence alleged and include supporting material such as statements, notices, photographs or other evidence. Check whether the documents appear complete and relate to the correct matter.

04

Hearing or adjournment notice

This gives information about a court date or a change to an existing hearing. Check the court address, time, case reference and any instructions about attendance.

05

Notice of conviction or sentence

This may confirm the court's decision, penalty points, fine, disqualification or other orders. If you believe something is wrong, seek prompt advice about the available legal options.

Check the deadline before anything else.

A Single Justice Procedure Notice normally gives you 21 days to respond. If you are unsure what to do, do not ignore the notice or wait until the final day to seek advice.

The process

What can happen after a motoring charge?

The route depends on the offence, the evidence, the plea and the court's directions. Not every case follows the same sequence.

Some cases are dealt with through the Single Justice Procedure. Others require one or more hearings at a magistrates’ court.

Talk through your documents
01

The allegation is issued

The prosecution paperwork identifies the offence alleged and the court process being used. Check the dates, personal details and allegation carefully.

02

A response or plea is required

The notice explains how to respond. The options and procedure depend on the document and the type of case. Do not guess which response is appropriate.

03

The court considers the case

Depending on the plea and procedure, a magistrate may consider written material or the case may proceed to a hearing where evidence and legal arguments are addressed.

04

A decision or sentence follows

The court may dismiss the case or, following conviction, impose a sentence. This can include a fine, penalty points, a driving disqualification or other orders depending on the offence.

05

Any further action is considered

If there is a concern about the decision, the available options may include an appeal or an application for the court to reopen the case. Strict procedural rules and time limits can apply.

Penalty points

Facing a totting-up disqualification?

If you accumulate 12 or more penalty points within a three-year period, the court will generally have to disqualify you under the totting-up provisions. The usual minimum disqualification is six months where there is no relevant previous disqualification.

The court must consider the relevant circumstances and the statutory rules. The dates of the offences and the status of existing endorsements matter. A simple total from an old letter may not tell the whole story.

In some circumstances, a driver may ask the court to consider exceptional hardship. This is not an automatic exemption and it is not enough simply to say that losing a licence would be inconvenient.

Penalty points within three years

This can trigger a totting-up disqualification. The usual first disqualification under this procedure is at least six months.

Previous disqualifications matter.

The minimum period can increase where a relevant previous disqualification falls within the statutory rules.

Check the dates, not just the total.

The dates on which offences were committed and the applicable endorsement periods need to be examined.

Exceptional hardship

When losing your licence would affect more than your daily routine.

An exceptional hardship application asks the court to consider whether a totting-up disqualification would cause hardship that goes beyond the ordinary consequences of losing the ability to drive.

The court considers the evidence and the circumstances of the individual case. The impact on employment, dependants or other people may be relevant, but no particular outcome is guaranteed.

The application must be prepared carefully. It should be truthful, supported by evidence where possible and presented in accordance with the court's requirements.

An exceptional hardship argument is not a way to erase penalty points or excuse the underlying offence. It is a specific legal argument considered by the court in a totting-up case.

Evidence may include:

A letter from an employer explaining the driving requirements of your role.
Details of how a disqualification could affect your employment or livelihood.
Evidence of caring responsibilities and the needs of dependants.
Information about realistic alternative transport arrangements.
Documents supporting any claimed impact on other people.
A clear and accurate account of the circumstances relied upon.
Other court matters

Not every motoring case is about penalty points.

Some allegations carry more serious consequences or require a different approach to evidence, preparation and representation.

MATTER 01

Driving while disqualified

An allegation that a person drove while a disqualification was in force. The dates, status of the ban and evidence of driving can be important.

MATTER 02

Drink and drug driving

These cases can involve technical evidence, procedure, testing, statutory limits and potentially serious driving consequences.

MATTER 03

Failing to identify a driver

A failure to provide driver information can be a separate offence. The notice, service, response and surrounding circumstances need review.

MATTER 04

Careless or dangerous driving

The allegation, collision evidence, witness accounts and prosecution material can all be significant in determining the issues in dispute.

MATTER 05

Mobile phone offences

The circumstances of the alleged use, available evidence and applicable offence provisions should be considered before responding.

MATTER 06

New driver licence revocation

A new driver's licence can be revoked after six or more penalty points within two years of passing the first driving test. The underlying conviction may need urgent attention.

After a court decision

Think the decision was wrong?

There may be legal routes to challenge a conviction or sentence, but the correct procedure depends on what happened and what decision you are challenging.

An appeal is not simply a second opportunity to tell the court that the outcome feels unfair. The grounds, evidence, procedure and applicable deadline all need to be considered.

In some circumstances, an application may be made for a magistrates’ court to reopen a case. That is a different procedure from an appeal and has its own requirements.

Important

Do not assume you have unlimited time.

Appeals from a magistrates’ court generally have short time limits. The relevant period and procedure depend on the type of decision and route being used. Obtain prompt advice from a qualified legal adviser before deciding what to do.

If you have already received a conviction or sentence notice, keep the document and note the date it was issued.

Common questions

Before you respond.

General answers to questions people often have when a motoring matter reaches court.

The magistrate may make a decision without hearing from you. You could be convicted and fined. The notice normally gives you 21 days to respond, so read it promptly and follow its instructions.
Reaching 12 or more points within the relevant three-year period generally triggers a totting-up disqualification. The court must apply the relevant legal rules and consider any properly raised exceptional hardship argument.
A court may decide not to impose the usual totting-up disqualification if the legal test is met. The outcome depends on the evidence and circumstances. It is not guaranteed.
If you receive six or more penalty points within two years of passing your first driving test, your licence will normally be revoked under the new-driver rules. You generally need to apply for a provisional licence and pass the theory and practical tests again.
There are procedures that may allow a magistrates’ court to reopen certain cases. Whether one applies depends on the facts and procedural requirements. Get legal advice quickly rather than assuming the conviction will automatically be cancelled.
